00Start here

Assume no restoration date and no outside help. Work the priorities in order. Everything below is for a 2 adult, 3 young child household in a rural Warren CountySedgwick County winter.

The three killers in a winter outage
  1. Carbon monoxide. Generators, grills, camp stoves, and gas ovens used to heat or cook indoors. Kills silently, often at night. See §6.
  2. Cold. Hypothermia in infants and toddlers starts before adults feel it. See §4 and §5.
  3. Fire. Candles, space heaters, overloaded cords, stove ash. Fire department response may be slow or absent.
Contaminated water (diarrheal illness, dehydration in small kids) is the fourth, and it builds over days, not hours.

Priority order

  1. Safety: CO alarms on battery, fire extinguisher located, everyone accounted for.
  2. Warmth: pick one warm room now, before the house cools. Close off the rest.
  3. Water: capture what is in the pipes and water heater before it freezes or drains (§3).
  4. Information: radio on, find out scope (local storm vs regional grid event).
  5. Food: eat fridge food first, keep freezer shut.
  6. Sanitation: set up the bucket toilet before you need it.
  7. Sustain: fuel, power rationing, hunting/foraging only once the above are stable.
Local context · Oregonia / Warren County
  • You are on the PJM grid (13 states plus DC). Western PJM includes Duke Energy Ohio, which serves much of Warren County. In January 2026 Winter Storm Fern pushed PJM into a Level 1 emergency and federal emergency orders; PJM has publicly warned demand could outrun supply as early as 2027. A cold snap is the most likely trigger for a long outage here.
  • If you are on a private well, your water stops when the power stops. The pump needs electricity. Treat water capture (§3) as a first hour task.
  • Nearby surface water: Little Miami River, Todd Fork, Caesar Creek Lake. The Little Miami is widely impaired by E. coli and receives agricultural runoff. Always filter and disinfect, and never use it for infant formula (§3).
Local context · Wichita / Sedgwick County, KS
  • Kansas is on the Southwest Power Pool (SPP) grid. Evergy serves Wichita; rural areas are often on electric co-ops. During Winter Storm Uri (February 2021), SPP ordered rotating outages across its region, including Wichita, because of extreme cold. Ice storms and arctic cold are the main winter outage triggers here; tornadoes and straight-line winds cause outages the rest of the year.
  • Wichita city water comes from Cheney Reservoir and the Equus Beds aquifer and needs electric pumps and treatment. Expect pressure loss or a boil advisory in a long outage. Private wells stop when the power stops. Capture water in the first hour (§3).
  • Nearby surface water: Arkansas River, Little Arkansas River, Cheney Reservoir, farm ponds. Summer and fall blue-green algae blooms (Cheney is a known site) make toxins that boiling, bleach and most filters do not remove: never use water that is green, scummy or paint-like. Farm runoff and urban runoff (PCBs on the Arkansas below Wichita) are also concerns. Filter and disinfect, and never use surface water for infant formula.
  • Wind chill is the cold multiplier on the plains: block wind first when setting up shelter and outdoor work.

01Outage timeline

What to do and when. Tick items in the Supply checklist (§18) beforehand so this list is execution, not shopping.

Turn off/unplug electronics and most breakers to avoid surge on restoration; leave one light circuit on so you know when power returns. Turn fridge and freezer to coldest setting if not already. Radio on. Phones to low power mode. Fill every container, tub, and pot from the taps while pressure remains.
Hour 1 to 6Choose the warm room (small, interior or south facing, with a door). Hang blankets over doorways and windows. Move bedding, kid supplies, water, lighting, and the bucket toilet nearby. Bring pets in. If temps will be below freezing: protect pipes (§4) or drain the house.
Day 1Eat refrigerated perishables first; fridge is safe about 4 hours closed. Start the cooking routine (outdoors or with a vented wood stove only). Inventory fuel, water, food, meds. Day 2 to 3Freezer limit: full freezer about 48 hours, half full about 24. Cook, can, or dry the meat before it thaws past safe. Check on neighbors, especially elderly and those on medical devices. Establish water treatment as a daily batch job. Ration generator fuel to defined windows (well pump, freezer pulls, charging).
Day 4 to 14Shift from stored to procured: hunting, trapping, fishing, foraging within your skill and the law. Formalize sanitation (latrine or composting buckets). Hygiene discipline matters more each day. Pool resources with a few trusted neighbors.
Beyond 2 weeksLong horizon: fuelwood supply, seed and garden planning for spring, meat preservation at scale, community watch, barter. Reassess whether to relocate to family or a better heated site while roads and fuel still allow it.

03Water: collection and purification

Store at least 1 gallon per person per day (CDC minimum). For five people that is 70 gallons for two weeks before any hygiene.

Sources inside the house (use first, in this order)

  1. Water already in containers you filled at Hour 0.
  2. Water heater tank (40 to 80 gal). Cut power (breaker) or set a gas unit to pilot/off first. Close the cold inlet valve on top. Open a hot tap somewhere in the house to let air in, then drain from the bottom valve into a clean container. First liter may have sediment; let it settle. This is safe drinking water if your supply was safe.
  3. House plumbing. Open the highest faucet, then drain from the lowest faucet or outside spigot.
  4. Toilet tank (the back tank, not the bowl): usable for flushing or, after disinfection, drinking if no tank cleaner tablets were ever used.
  5. Ice cubes, canned fruit and vegetable liquid.

Sources outside

  • Snow and ice: melt first, then disinfect. Do not eat snow to hydrate; it drops body temperature. Collect fresh snow away from roads and roofs treated with chemicals.
  • Rain and roof runoff: discard the first flush of each rain, then filter and disinfect. Asphalt shingles add debris and chemicals; prefer a tarp or clean metal roof catchment.
  • Streams, river, lake: highest risk. Settle, prefilter, filter, then disinfect.
Surface water and agriculture Boiling and bleach kill germs. They do not remove farm chemicals, fuel, or nitrates. Boiling actually concentrates nitrates, which are dangerous to infants. In farm country after rain or snowmelt, runoff is worst.
  • Activated carbon filters help with many pesticides, fuel and taste, but not nitrates.
  • Infants and formula: use stored bottled water or water drawn from your own system before the outage. Ready to feed formula is the best emergency option because it needs no water at all. Never mix formula with river, pond, or ditch water.
  • Avoid water with an oily sheen, fuel smell, or near flooded fields, barns, or septic fields.

Treatment sequence

  1. Settle: let cloudy water stand in a bucket for hours; pour off the clear top.
  2. Prefilter: through a clean cloth, coffee filter, or sand/charcoal layered bucket. This makes chemical disinfection work.
  3. Filter: a 0.1 to 0.2 micron hollow fiber filter (Sawyer, LifeStraw family, ceramic) removes bacteria and protozoa like Giardia and Cryptosporidium. Most do not stop viruses. Hollow fiber filters are ruined if they freeze when wet; keep them in a pocket or the warm room.
  4. Disinfect by one of these:
    • Boil: rolling boil for 1 minute. Most reliable method. Costs fuel.
    • Bleach (5 to 9% unscented): 8 drops per gallon clear water, 16 drops if cloudy or very cold. 8.25% bleach: 6 drops clear, 12 cloudy. Stir, wait 30 minutes. You should smell a faint chlorine odor; if not, repeat the dose and wait 15 more minutes. Use the calculator in §2.
    • Pool shock (calcium hypochlorite, 65 to 78%, no algaecide or clarifier): stores for years, far better shelf life than bleach. EPA method: dissolve one heaping teaspoon (about ¼ oz) in 2 gallons of water to make a stock solution. Then add 1 part stock to 100 parts water to be treated (about 6½ fl oz, a bit over ¾ cup, per 5 gallons; 1 pint per 12½ gallons). Wait 30 minutes. Store the granules sealed, cool, and away from fuels and acids; it is a strong oxidizer and fire risk.
    • Chlorine dioxide tablets: effective against Crypto with longer contact time; follow the package.
  5. Store treated water in clean, covered food grade containers. Label "TREATED" with the date.
Taste fix Flat tasting boiled water: pour back and forth between two clean containers, or let it stand a few hours. Add a pinch of salt per quart if needed.

Storage notes

  • Use food grade containers (blue barrels, water bricks, commercially bottled). Not milk jugs (they degrade and leak).
  • Stored tap water: rotate every 6 months. Commercial bottled water: follow the date.
  • In freezing temps, store water in the warm room, or leave headspace so frozen jugs do not split. Frozen jugs in a cooler double as refrigeration.

04Heat and shelter (winter)

Heat people, not the house. A small sealed room with five bodies, insulation, and a safe heat source can hold livable temperatures for days.

Set up the warm room

  • Pick a small room with few windows, ideally south facing and interior. One door, closable.
  • Close every other room. Stuff towels at door gaps. Hang blankets or quilts over windows and doorways; tack plastic sheeting over windows (trapped air layer). Open south curtains on sunny days, close at sunset.
  • Put foam pads, rugs, or cardboard on the floor; cold floors pull heat fast.
  • Make a room within a room: a tent pitched indoors, or a blanket canopy over a table. The smaller the air volume around sleeping people, the warmer.
  • Everyone sleeps together in the warm room. Hats on at night. Sleeping bags rated for cold, inside the tent.

Clothing

  • Layers: wicking base (wool or synthetic, never cotton next to skin in cold), insulating middle (fleece, down, wool), wind/water shell outside.
  • Stay dry. Sweat is the enemy. Strip a layer before working hard, add it back when you stop.
  • Change into dry socks before bed. Keep tomorrow's clothes in the sleeping bag.
  • Eat and drink well. Calories are fuel for body heat; dehydration speeds cold injury.

Pipes and plumbing

  • Short outage: open sink cabinet doors, let a cold faucet drip on exterior walls. Disconnect and drain outdoor hoses; close interior valves to hose bibs.
  • Long outage with the house going below freezing: drain the system. Shut the main valve (or well pump breaker), open all faucets top floor to bottom, flush toilets, drain the water heater (save that water, §3). Pour RV antifreeze (non toxic, propylene glycol) into sink and tub drains and toilet bowls to protect traps.
  • Frozen pipe: open the faucet, warm the pipe from the faucet end back with warm towels or a hair dryer on a generator. Never use an open flame.

05Cold injuries and young kids

Babies and small children lose heat much faster than adults and cannot tell you. Check them, do not wait for complaints.

Hypothermia: adults

  • Shivering, exhaustion
  • Confusion, fumbling hands
  • Memory loss, slurred speech
  • Drowsiness

Below 95°F (35°C) body temperature is an emergency. Hypothermia can happen above 40°F if someone is wet.

Hypothermia: babies and toddlers

  • Bright red, cold skin
  • Very low energy, floppy, hard to wake
  • Poor feeding, weak cry

Feel the chest and back of the neck, not the hands, to judge core warmth.

Frostbite

  • Redness or pain first
  • White or grayish yellow skin
  • Unusually firm or waxy feel, numbness

Fingers, toes, ears, nose, cheeks.

Rewarming

  1. Get the person into the warm room. Remove wet clothing.
  2. Warm the core first: chest, neck, head, groin. Use skin to skin contact under loose, dry blankets. For a child, a parent's bare chest inside a sleeping bag is ideal.
  3. Warm, sweet, non alcoholic drinks if fully awake. Nothing by mouth if drowsy or unconscious.
  4. If no pulse or breathing, start CPR and keep going while warming. People in severe hypothermia have been revived after appearing dead.

Frostbite care

  • Do not rub or massage, and never rub with snow.
  • Do not walk on frostbitten feet unless you must.
  • Rewarm in warm, not hot, water (comfortable to an unaffected hand). Do not rewarm if there is any chance of refreezing; refreeze damage is worse.
  • Do not use heaters, fires, or heating pads on numb skin; it burns without being felt.

Kids in a cold house

  • Infant sleep: keep the safe sleep rules even in the cold. Baby on back, own firm surface, no loose blankets, pillows, or bumpers. Add warmth with layers and a wearable blanket or sleep sack, plus a hat for sleep only in a cold room, and keep the crib or bassinet in the warm room. One more layer than an adult is comfortable in is a good rule.
  • Check hands, feet, ears, and energy every few hours and at every diaper change.
  • Keep small kids well fed and drinking. Toddlers burn through calories fast in cold.
  • Combustion heaters: barrier around them. Kids and pets out of the 3 ft zone.
  • Keep routines (meals, story, bedtime). It cuts stress for everyone.
  • Diarrhea in a small child is an emergency without power. Start oral rehydration early (§13).

06Fire, carbon monoxide and generators

Carbon monoxide Colorless, odorless. More than 400 Americans die each year from non fire CO poisoning. Symptoms: headache, dizziness, weakness, nausea, vomiting, chest pain, confusion. Several people sick at once, or symptoms that improve outside, means CO. Get everyone outside into fresh air immediately.
  • Battery or battery backup CO alarm on every level and in the warm room. Spare batteries.

Generator rules

  • Outdoors only. Never in the house, basement, crawl space, shed, or attached garage, even with doors open.
  • At least 20 feet from any window, door, or vent (CDC). NIST modeling found 15 feet was not always enough for a two story house; 25 to 30 feet with the exhaust pointed away from the house performed much better. Calm, still nights are the worst case.
  • Use a heavy gauge outdoor extension cord longer than 20 feet.
  • Prefer units with automatic CO shutoff (certified to PGMA G300 or UL 2201).
  • Keep dry: run under an open canopy, not enclosed.
  • Refuel only when cool. Store fuel in approved cans away from the house.
  • Never backfeed the house by plugging the generator into a wall outlet. It can energize the utility lines and kill line workers and can burn your house. Use a transfer switch or a code compliant interlock installed by an electrician.

Fire

  • Use LED lanterns and headlamps, not candles. If candles are unavoidable: stable holder, away from anything that burns, never unattended, never near kids.
  • Fire extinguishers (ABC) in kitchen, warm room, and near the wood stove. Everyone knows where.
  • Escape plan from the warm room. Kids' window route known.
  • Do not overload extension cords. No daisy chaining power strips.
  • Smoke alarms tested; spare 9V and AA batteries.

07Food and cooking

Use food in this order

  1. Refrigerator perishables: safe about 4 hours with the door shut. After 4 hours above 40°F, discard meat, poultry, fish, eggs, soft cheese, milk, leftovers.
  2. Freezer: full about 48 hours, half full about 24, door shut. Fifty pounds of dry ice keeps a full 18 cu ft freezer cold about 2 days. Food that still has ice crystals or is at 40°F or below can be refrozen.
  3. Pantry shelf stable foods.
Winter "free refrigeration" trap USDA warns against just putting food outside in snow: sun can warm it even on cold days, temperatures swing, and animals get into it. Better: fill jugs with water, freeze them outside overnight, and put them in coolers or the fridge. A cooler in a shaded, unheated garage or on a north porch, checked with a thermometer, is far safer than a snowbank.

Calories

Plan roughly 2,000 to 2,500 kcal per adult per day in cold weather (more if hauling wood or hunting), and about 1,000 to 1,600 for young children depending on age. Fat matters in the cold and with lean game (see rabbit starvation in §8).

What to stock (low effort, long shelf life)

  • Rice, oats, pasta, flour, dried beans and lentils (beans need lots of fuel; canned beans are fuel efficient).
  • Canned meat, fish, soups, vegetables, fruit. Peanut butter, nuts, honey, sugar, salt, cooking oil, shortening.
  • Powdered milk, ready to feed infant formula, baby food, toddler snacks.
  • Multivitamins. Coffee and tea (morale).
  • Manual can opener (two).

Cooking without power

  • Outdoors: propane grill, charcoal grill, camp stove, rocket stove, fire pit. Under an open overhang at most, never inside or in an attached garage.
  • Indoors: only a properly vented wood stove or fireplace.
  • Save fuel: one pot meals, lids on, soak beans and grains overnight, retained heat cooking (bring to a boil, then wrap the pot in blankets or a cooler for a few hours).
  • Use a food thermometer: game and ground meat to 160°F, poultry and game birds to 165°F.

Universal handling rules

  • Never harvest an animal acting sick: stumbling, unafraid, emaciated, drooling, circling, or found dead. Rabbits with tularemia often run slowly and are easy to catch; that is a warning sign, not luck.
  • Gloves (nitrile or rubber) for all field dressing, especially rabbits and rodents. Cover cuts on your hands. Do not touch eyes or mouth.
  • Gut quickly and cool fast. Heat and bacteria spoil meat, not time alone. In winter, an opened deer cools well hanging in shade at 34 to 40°F.
  • Inspect organs. Discard the animal if the liver has white or yellow spots, there are abscesses, or the meat smells off.
  • Cook all wild game thoroughly: 160°F for game meat and ground meat, 165°F for game birds.
  • Wash hands, knives, and surfaces with soap and treated water; sanitize with a bleach solution.
  • Kids should not handle sick or dead animals.

Deer: field dressing step by step

  1. Confirm dead. Approach from behind, touch the eye with a stick. Tag per current law.
  2. Position on its back, head slightly uphill. Gloves on.
  3. Cut around the anus (core it out), free it a few inches, and tie it off with string so feces does not contaminate meat.
  4. Open the belly: pinch the skin below the breastbone, make a small cut, insert two fingers in a V with the knife edge up between them, and cut toward the pelvis. Do not puncture the stomach or intestines.
  5. Free the organs: reach up into the chest, cut the diaphragm along the ribs, reach forward and sever the windpipe and esophagus as far up as you can.
  6. Roll out the guts: pull the windpipe back and down; the whole package should come out, pulling the cored anus through the pelvis. Save the heart and liver if they look clean (dark red, smooth, no spots).
  7. Drain and cool: tip the deer to drain blood. Prop the cavity open with a stick. Keep it out of sun.
  8. Hang by the hind legs (gambrel) or head in a cool, shaded spot, 34 to 40°F ideal. Skin soon if above 40°F; skinning speeds cooling. If it will freeze hard, skinning early avoids a frozen hide.
  9. Skin and quarter: cut around the hind legs above the hocks, slit down the inside of each leg, peel the hide down. Remove front shoulders (no joint, just cut along the ribcage), backstraps along the spine, tenderloins inside the cavity, then hindquarters at the hip joint. Neck and rib meat for stew or grind.
  10. Trim bloodshot meat, hair, fat (deer fat tastes bad), silverskin. Wrap or process the same day if above freezing.
Use the whole animal Bones: boil for stock (fat and calories). Hide: brain tan or salt for later use. Fat: render beef style tallow for cooking and candles. Hooves and sinew: cordage. Hide and offal not used: bury deep or bag and dispose far from the house to avoid drawing scavengers.

Small game: rabbit and squirrel

  1. Gloves on. Pinch the back skin, cut a small slit across the middle of the back.
  2. Hook fingers in, pull one half toward the head and one toward the tail. Hide peels off like a sock.
  3. Cut off head, feet and tail. Open the belly carefully, remove organs. Inspect the liver.
  4. Rinse with treated water. Quarter: front legs, hind legs, saddle.
  5. Cook thoroughly; braise or stew older animals.

Raccoon, groundhog and opossum: shared rules

Before you touch one
  • Rabies: raccoons are a major rabies carrier in Ohio,skunks are the main rabies carrier in Kansas; raccoons and other mammals can also carry it, and groundhogs account for most rabid rodent cases in the eastern US. Pass on any animal that is out in daylight acting tame, aggressive, wobbly, or paralyzed. Do not cut into the head, and do not eat the brain.
  • Trichinella: omnivores and carnivores can carry it. Wild strains can survive home freezing, so freezing is not a substitute for cooking. Cook every piece to 160°F all the way through.
  • Gloves, eye protection, no kids nearby. Wash hands and tools with soap, then sanitize with bleach solution.
  • Fleas and ticks leave a cooling carcass. Bag it for 30 minutes or hang it away from the house before you skin it.
  • Fat is the flavor problem on all three. Trim it hard. Parboiling (simmer 20 to 30 min in salted water, then discard the water) before roasting or stewing removes gamey taste and tenderizes older animals.

Raccoon: step by step

Raccoon roundworm Baylisascaris eggs are shed in raccoon droppings and can get on fur. If swallowed, they can cause severe brain and eye damage, and young children are most at risk. Skin outdoors, on a surface you can sanitize or burn, never in the kitchen. Gloves on start to finish. Avoid the anus and intestines.
  1. Hang by the hind legs from a gambrel at chest height.
  2. Ring cut around each hind leg just above the ankle. Slit the skin down the inside of each leg to the vent. Cut around the vent without opening the gut.
  3. Peel down. Work the hide down over the body, using the knife only to free connective tissue. For meat only, you can also open the hide down the belly and peel it off sideways. To save the pelt, case skin it (pull it off whole, inside out, like a sock). Cut off the front feet and head with the hide.
  4. Remove the scent ("kernel") glands before gutting. They are small, pea to bean sized, waxy gray, brown, or reddish lumps buried in the fat: in each armpit, on the inside of each hind thigh, and along the small of the back. Cut around them with fat attached; do not slice into them. Tainted meat tastes and smells bad.
  5. Gut. Cut through the belly wall shallowly from pelvis to breastbone, knife edge up. Roll out the guts without puncturing the bladder or intestines. Discard the organs unless they are clearly healthy; most people skip raccoon offal.
  6. Trim all fat. Raccoon fat is heavy and strong. Render it separately for soap, leather, or lamp fuel rather than eating it.
  7. Quarter: two hindquarters (the best meat), two front legs, and the backstraps. Rinse in treated water.
  8. Cook: parboil, then roast, braise, or slow cook with onions and acid (vinegar, tomato) to 160°F. Tastes like dark, rich pork or mutton.
  9. Dispose: bag and bury the hide scraps, guts, and head deep, or burn. Sanitize the work surface with bleach. Roundworm eggs resist most disinfectants, so a burned or discarded board is safer than a scrubbed one.

Groundhog (woodchuck): step by step

  1. Choose young (under about 6 lb) for tender meat. Big old males need long braising.
  2. Hang by the hind legs. Ring cut above the ankles, slit down the inside legs, and peel the hide down like a raccoon. The hide is tight on the back; use the knife to free it. A belly cut and peel also works.
  3. Remove the glands: one in each armpit under the front legs and small glands near the base of the tail / small of the back. They look like the raccoon's, small and waxy in the fat. Cut them out whole.
  4. Gut as above, avoiding the bladder. A clean liver and heart can be kept.
  5. Trim the fat. Fall groundhogs carry a lot of it. It is strong when roasted but usable rendered.
  6. Quarter like a rabbit: front legs, hind legs, saddle.
  7. Cook: parboil, then fry, roast, or stew to 160°F. Mild and dark, often compared to rabbit crossed with pork.

Winter note: groundhogs hibernate from roughly late fall to February, so expect them mostly in fall and from late winter on.

Opossum: step by step

  1. Consider "cleaning" first. Opossums eat carrion and garbage. The traditional practice is to live trap one and keep it caged on clean food (corn, grain, fruit, scraps) and water for 1 to 2 weeks before butchering. A wild opossum taken directly is still edible if processed and cooked properly.
  2. Risks: rabies is rare in opossums, but they can carry leptospirosis (spread through urine) along with fleas and ticks. Gloves on, and keep cuts covered.
  3. Scald or skin. The hide is thin and peels easily. Hang by the hind legs, ring cut, slit the inside legs, and case skin it like a raccoon. Traditional alternative: scald in about 160°F water and scrape off the hair like a hog, which keeps the skin on.
  4. Remove the musk glands located near the anus. Cut out the whole vent area with the glands attached, without cutting into them.
  5. Gut carefully, then trim the heavy layer of fat, especially along the back and belly.
  6. Cook: parboil (some change the water twice), then roast with sweet potatoes, the classic Southern way, or stew. Cook to 160°F. Greasy, dark meat. It's best for stews, where fat cooks off.

Birds

  • Breasting out (fast): pluck a patch over the breastbone, cut the skin, peel back, slice the meat off each side of the keel. Take legs for slow cooking.
  • Plucking: scald larger birds at about 150°F for about a minute, then feathers pull easily. Ducks: dip in hot water with melted paraffin, cool, peel off the wax and down.
  • Gut through a cut below the breastbone; remove crop at the neck.

Trapping basics

  • Ohio requires a fur taker permitKansas requires a furharvester license and has specific trap rules (check daily, identification, legal types). Snares on land have restrictions; confirm before setting.
  • Body grip and cage traps on trails, den entrances, and creek crossings. Rabbit and squirrel: box traps baited with apple or corn.
  • Trapping runs 24/7 and frees you for other work, which is its real value in a long outage.

09Fishing

  • Waters nearby: Little Miami River (smallmouth bass, rock bass, channel catfish, sunfish), Caesar Creek Lake (crappie, bass, saugeye, catfish, bluegill), Todd Fork and farm ponds (bluegill, bass, catfish with permission).
  • Waters nearby: Cheney Reservoir (walleye, white bass, wipers, crappie, channel catfish), El Dorado Lake, the Arkansas and Little Arkansas Rivers (catfish, carp, white bass), and countless farm ponds (bluegill, bass, catfish with permission).
  • Winter methods: slow, deep presentation. Catfish on cut bait or worms on the bottom; crappie and bluegill on small jigs or minnows near cover. Trotlines and set lines fish while you sleep (check OhioKansas limits on hooks and tagging).
  • Ice: only when solid ice is at least 4 inches, and never on rivers or near inlets, springs, or the dam. Southwest OhioSouth-central Kansas ice is often unreliable. Carry ice picks and a rope; never go alone.
  • Contaminants: Ohio EPA publishes fish consumption advisories (mercury, PCBs). Generally favor smaller panfish over large old catfish and carp; limit meals for kids. Check the advisory list in advance.
  • Contaminants (KDHE 2026 advisories): Arkansas River from the Lincoln Street dam in Wichita down to Cowskin Creek near Belle Plaine: do not eat bottom-feeding fish (carp, catfish, buffalo; PCBs). Little Arkansas River in Wichita: bottom feeders 2 servings a month max. K-96 Lake: 1 a month. Statewide mercury: channel catfish, crappie, walleye, sunfish 1 to 2 servings a week; largemouth bass, flathead catfish, buffalo 1 to 2 a month; cut these in half for fish over 20 inches. Children and pregnant women follow the limits strictly. Child serving sizes: 4 oz (ages 6 to 12), 2 oz (under 6).
  • Prep: scale or skin, gut, remove the dark mud line on catfish. Cook to 145°F or until flaky. Crayfish: boil thoroughly.
  • Fishing license required (age 16 and older; Kansas residents 75 and older exempt).

11Preserving meat without power

  • Cold storage (winter only): quartered meat in game bags hung in an unheated outbuilding or garage that stays 28 to 40°F, checked with a thermometer twice a day. Protect from animals and sun. Fails the moment a warm spell hits.
  • Pressure canning: the only safe home canning method for meat and low acid foods. Water bath canning is NOT safe for meat. Run the canner on a propane burner outdoors. Follow tested USDA or Ball times and pressures for your altitude (Warren County is under 1,000 ft).
  • Jerky: slice lean meat thin (¼ inch, across the grain), trim all fat (fat goes rancid). USDA guidance: heat meat to 160°F before or at the start of drying, then dry at 130 to 140°F until it cracks but does not break when bent. Use a smoker, a low oven on a generator, or a wood stove setup with a thermometer.
  • Smoking: hot smoking cooks and partially preserves (short term). Cold smoking alone does not make meat safe without a cure.
  • Salting and curing: dry cures need correct salt ratios and curing salt (sodium nitrite) to prevent botulism. Stock Prague Powder #1 and #2 and a tested recipe if you plan this.
  • Pemmican: dried meat pounded to powder, mixed roughly 1:1 by weight with rendered fat. Very calorie dense and long keeping if fully dried and fat is clean.
  • Rendered fat (tallow, lard): strain, store in clean jars, keeps months in cool storage.

12Sanitation and hygiene

In past disasters, poor sanitation caused more illness than injuries. Diarrhea in small children is dangerous without medical care.

Toilets

  • Gravity septic with water: flush by pouring a bucket of non drinking water (2 to 3 gal) quickly into the bowl. Do this sparingly; "if it's yellow, let it mellow."
  • Pumped or aerobic septic, or no water: do not flush.
  • Bucket toilet: 5 gal bucket, heavy duty liners (double bag), toilet seat lid. Cover each use with sawdust, cat litter, peat, or wood ash. Tie off and store bags in a lidded can outside until disposal. Keep urine separate if possible (less smell, less volume).
  • Long term: a latrine trench at least 200 ft from any well or water source and downhill from it. Cover each use with soil.

Hygiene

  • Hand washing station: jug with spigot, soap, catch bucket. Wash before food and after toilet, diapers, and game. Sanitizer is a backup, not a replacement.
  • Dish washing: wash, rinse, then sanitize in a bleach solution (about 1 tbsp unscented bleach per gallon), air dry.
  • Diapers: stock disposables deep; cloth requires lots of hot water.
  • Sponge baths in the warm room. Keep feet clean and dry.
  • Trash: bag, seal, store outside away from the house. Burn only paper and wood. Bury food scraps or compost to avoid scavengers.

13Medical

Limits of this page This is reference only, not training. Take a hands-on first aid, CPR, and Stop the Bleed class, and pediatric CPR in particular.

Oral rehydration solution (WHO style home recipe)

1 liter (about 1 quart) treated water + 6 level teaspoons sugar + ½ level teaspoon salt. Stir until dissolved. Sip small amounts often. For infants and toddlers, commercial ORS (Pedialyte type) is preferred; stock several. Danger signs in kids: no urine for 8 hours (fewer wet diapers in babies), sunken eyes, no tears, very sleepy, sunken soft spot. Those need a doctor if any path to one exists.

Bleeding

  1. Firm direct pressure with gauze or cloth. Do not lift to peek.
  2. Pack deep wounds with gauze and press.
  3. Life threatening limb bleeding: commercial tourniquet (CAT or SOF-T) high and tight, note the time.

Wounds and infection

  • Irrigate cuts with lots of treated water. Clean, cover, change dressings daily.
  • Watch for spreading redness, heat, swelling, pus, fever, red streaks. Infection without antibiotics is serious.
  • Burns: cool water 10 to 20 minutes, cover loosely, no butter or ice.

Medications

  • Keep a written list of everyone's prescriptions, doses, allergies, and doctors.
  • Build a 30 day buffer of essential prescriptions where legally possible.
  • Insulin and some meds need cool storage: a cooler with frozen jugs, kept in the coldest safe spot. Do not let insulin freeze.
  • Children's pain and fever reducers: dose strictly by the package label for the child's weight and the specific product. Keep the boxes with their dosing charts.

Kit essentials

Gauze, roller bandages, trauma dressing, tourniquet, tape, nitrile gloves, saline or irrigation syringe, butterfly closures, antibiotic ointment, burn gel, thermometers (including a rectal/infant thermometer), tweezers, scissors, SAM splint, ORS packets, pain and fever reducers (adult and children's), antihistamine, antidiarrheal (adults), hydrocortisone cream, a printed first aid manual.

14Power and lighting

Load priority (if you have any power)

  1. Well pump (in bursts, fill containers then shut off)
  2. Freezer and fridge (run a few hours, then off; keep doors shut)
  3. Furnace blower for a gas furnace (needs a transfer switch; a gas furnace will not run without electricity)
  4. Medical devices, phone and radio charging, lights
  5. Comfort loads last

Fuel

  • Gasoline degrades in months; add stabilizer and rotate into vehicles every 6 to 12 months. Store in approved cans, detached from the house.
  • Gas station pumps need power. Keep vehicles above half a tank all winter.
  • Propane stores indefinitely and is the best long storage generator and cooking fuel. Dual fuel generators are worth it.
  • Track run hours and fuel burn so you can forecast how many days you have.

Quiet power

  • Portable power stations (lithium) plus folding solar panels run lights, phones, radios, and CPAPs silently with no CO. Winter sun is weak; size panels generously.
  • A vehicle can charge devices with an inverter. Run it only outdoors with the tailpipe clear of snow.

Light

  • Headlamps for every person (hands free), LED lanterns for the warm room, glow sticks for kids at night.
  • Rechargeable AA/AAA with a USB or solar charger; one battery size across devices simplifies everything.

  • NOAA Weather Radio with battery and hand crank. Program the SAME code for Sedgwick County, Kansas: 020173. Transmitter KEC-59 Wichita, 162.550 MHz, from NWS Wichita.

16Security and community

  • Neighbors are your best asset. Know who has a generator, wood stove, medical training, tools, livestock, or a well with a hand pump. Agree in advance to check on each other.
  • Low profile: do not advertise supplies. A lit, warm, generator humming house stands out on a dark road. Blackout curtains after dark; run the generator in daylight when possible.
  • Keep doors locked, vehicles locked, fuel and tools out of sight.
  • Have a plan for who answers the door and how. Children stay out of sight of strangers.
  • Firearms: secured from children at all times, especially with stress and darkness. Ammunition stored separately.
  • Barter goods with value: fuel, batteries, coffee, salt, medicine, ammunition, skills.
